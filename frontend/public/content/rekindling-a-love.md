# Rekindling A Love

## 6/5/25 - My Relationship With Running

It was the first hot day of summer today — the first day over 85 degrees. I was walking home from the train at dusk, wading through the abusively familiar and slightly inconvenient mugginess of the NYC summer heat. I was walking down the sidewalk of a residential, single-lane, one-way road. It was a very quiet street for NYC standards. The buildings surrounding me were only a few stories high, but they lined my vision to the left and right. There were no gaps between them… nothing out of the ordinary for North Brooklyn. I felt the slightly-more-than-mild heat radiating from the pavement and the buildings of the narrow street. I felt the dewiness from my sweat gathering on my face and on my arms.

The sky was a warm gradient. While the sun had not fallen over the horizon yet, I could no longer see it directly over the tops of the tightly packed city homes. It was blue outside.

…On a nice summer NYC day, there's a moment where it's still bright outside. You can see the sunlight bouncing off the clouds and the sky ablaze with gold and red and pink and orange. The sky is on fire. But because of the buildings, you can't see the sun itself. If you can manage to look down from the alluring warm palette of the sky, and look to ground level, where you're standing, you'll notice it's completely juxtaposed from what you see above you. It's actually all just shadow. It's all blue.

---

I was walking down 6th and Roebling, but the scene reminded me of another place.

In my mind, I saw another beautiful sky on fire, and everything around me was blue.

## COVID

In 2020, I fell in love with running and endurance sport. Just look at Peloton's stock if you want to see how much a Sheeple I am.

Like many others, I locked myself away, alone in a small NYC apartment. I went months without interacting with other people. The city was empty. The only sounds I'd hear from outside were the sounds of sirens from emergency response teams blaring in the otherwise silent streets.

I felt very very very alone and struggled with deeply depressive states during the time. I was struggling a lot with the isolation and the state of the world.

However, it wasn't my first time dealing with something like this. A common wisdom that I subscribe to is to make sure you're exercising if you're feeling down. It's something that had worked for me in the past, and I was already a huge fan of staying active. It's kind of a magic bullet for me.

COVID changed that for me. The gym, playing sports with friends, going on hikes. These all disappeared as options for me. I felt so isolated, and I felt hopeless without a release.

Eventually, I decided to order some running shoes and give this foreign and seemingly unappealing activity a shot. I thought running was stupid, and I was fucking terrible at it too.

But I just wanted to feel better. I started running not out of interest, but out of necessity.

## Chasing the Dragon

I was addicted from the first week. I've never been addicted to something like I've been addicted to running.

Everyday, I found myself thinking about nothing but the next run. It was the only thing that brought me any happiness throughout the day. Running forced my mind and body to feel happy and in the moment. It was purely a physiological response.

I had no running background, and my body was not equipped for what I was doing. Even running a few miles in a week was a lot for me at the time, but I was doing way more than that. I was so weak physically, but I just wanted to feel better.

I remember my bones feeling bruised, my joints aching, my calves tight. Most distinctly, I remember waking up in the middle of the night with my heart thumping in my chest and feeling extremely stressed from the cortisol ripping through my body. I'd get dozens of cramps in a single run and just keep running. I'd wake up and barely be able to walk because my feet and legs were so fucked up. Despite that, the physical pain was nothing compared to the emotional numbness that I had when I wasn't running. Also, to be honest, I didn't realize what amount of pain was or wasn't normal for runners at the time. I thought maybe the pain was normal. My body was telling me to stop, but I didn't know how to listen. I didn't want to listen either. (also every run was a speed workout lol)

I started breaking up my runs into shorter distances, maybe even just 1-2 miles. It wasn't to give my body a break though. It was because I wanted to run more frequently. I couldn't go 24 hours without getting a hit. I wanted to run every few waking hours. I constantly needed an excuse to go outside and force my brain chemistry to feel better. I was often running 3 or 4 times a day. I remember I found a tiny grass patch near my apartment and I'd run circles just to run on a softer surface than the NYC pavement, so I could keep my joints from screaming at me.

I would get into bed at 2AM, and be unable to fall asleep because of the itch. I'd have to get myself back up, get dressed and go for a run in the pitch black darkness. I'd be the only person outside, besides the cops. They probably thought I was crazy. This Korean boy running circles on this tiny grass patch, across the street from them, in the middle of the night.

Rest days would be torture. I didn't really know how to take care of myself. I didn't care about getting faster or running farther. I'd use those metrics as excuses, but really I just wanted to run more, run as frequently as possible, and "feel better".

I'd describe my relationship with this typically healthy habit as completely and utterly unhealthy.

But it worked. It helped me survive through this period.

I am also very appreciative for having explored NYC when there were no people. It was surreal seeing all the sights and streets empty. I'd run in the middle of the roads by myself. It was a luxury to be honest!

## Colorado

I continued this behavior for months. Around July, I moved out of NYC and moved to Colorado, but I kept running.

Colorado fucking sucked. I had roommates, but we didn't end up getting along at all. I got really fucking tired of looking at rocks. Craft beers & hiking 14ers is nice once or twice, but living there was not for me. I did not have any friends or family around me. My roommates weren't bad people, I was just not in the right headspace to befriend them.

What I realized was that by moving to CO, I had placed myself in an even more isolating situation. I was geographically farther away from my friends or family, and I did not want to get on a plane or a bus because of COVID risk. I felt completely trapped there. It fuuuucccckkkkkingggggg sucked — probably my least favorite period of life.

I found myself super irritable with my roommates and I had very little regard for maintaining a healthy personal relationship with them.

All I did was run and work. I found a dirt trail and just ran up and down and up and down it every day.

About 2 months into my time in Colorado, I hurt myself. I sprained my Achilles, and I couldn't run. I could barely limp around the house. I was like this for about 3 or 4 weeks. I couldn't get my hit anymore.

During that time, I fell back into a deeply depressive state. It was worse than ever. The numbness and isolation returned. I was unable to feel any emotions. I remember thinking that if I were to die tomorrow, it'd be fine, because it'd be the same as what I was feeling now. I remember my roommates being worried that I was going to commit suicide.

I tried setting up regular video calls with friends, but it felt hard to talk about such personal things when everyone was struggling during this time. I also tried to explore outlets like virtual therapy, but didn't like the options I was seeing.

I somehow made it through that period of my life, relatively unscathed. I ended up working, watching TV, and reading books about how to not strangle my roommates or myself.

## San Diego

In October, we moved from Colorado to San Diego to join a 12 person "COVID bubble".

We met up with more friends, including Amit and Alex! (Izzy also visited!)

My ankle recovered, my isolation problem was highly alleviated, and I found myself happy again.

In San Diego, my relationship with running changed. It changed from an addiction and coping mechanism to a deep love and appreciation. I would go for a run every day or two. I started thinking about increasing distances and shooting for goals. I thought about balance between activity and recovery.

I also started running with my friends sometimes!

I started acknowledging that running might have saved me in many senses during the year of 2020, but that reflection also highlighted my weaknesses. It taught me a lot about my own mental and behavioral health.

Now, I am much healthier, more stable, and more aware because of my 2020.

I don't run that much anymore. I've been mostly going to the gym and playing tennis for the last 2-3 years. I've also been dealing with some injuries. Despite that, I still love and miss running, and my goal for this 60 day dinner is to tap back into that energy.

When I was walking down 6th and Roebling, it reminded me of these photos and videos — Izzy and a few other friends had come down to visit San Diego, towards the end of our time there. I was very happy during this time, and I am so grateful to have landed in a position like that after the dark period of deep COVID and Colorado. The 180 was so tangible for myself.

In those moments, the sky was on FIRE. I remember seeing only the silhouettes of my friends. We were running with not a care in the world! It was childish fun. They were blue shadows juxtaposed against the sky ablaze. However, they were down at ground level with me and they were all that I saw in that moment.
